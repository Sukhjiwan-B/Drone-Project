# Drone-Project
This is a project I've been building with 2 of my friends. Our current goal is autonomous flight, to get the drone to ascend to a certain height, hover for a set time and descend back to the ground. 


**Our process:**
We first started with research, and split the tasks among the 3 of us. I was responsible with learning about how to program the flight controller, my friend's task was learning about the hardware, and my other friend researched about the frame. Through this research we all learned about the parts that we needed and finalized our part selection and size of the drone together. Once we got our parts we began designing a frame to fit all the parts and soldered most the parts together that needed it. The flight controller was also programmed during this time. Once our prototype was assembled we began running smaller programs that were designed to help us gather data about the drone such as the PWM signal we needed to obtain a hover position. This is also where we reached out to a mentor to get advice on our drone on how we could improve it. From here we began a process of trial and error and learned a lot and had to design a new prototype that we are currently working on. This is a very brief summary of out process the next sections describe all the steps in more detail. 


**Part selection:**
The main parts we needed included a Power Distribution Board(PDB), 4 Electronic Speed Controllers(ESCs), 4 brushless motors + mounts, Propellors, LiPo Battery, MPU-6050 Internal Measurement Unit (IMU), ultrasonic sensor, wiring and a Microcontroller (We chose Arduino). Along with these parts there were tools we needed like a Soldering Iron, Multimeter, Legos for our prototype frame, Plywood for our version 2 (V2) frame, hand tools, screws and bolts to build that V2 frame. Our PDB is quite self explanatory but it directly connected to our battery and has our 4 ESCs connected to it along with our Arduino. The PDB takes the full 11.1 volts from our battery and distributes it to our ESCs which will control our motors and with the built in 5 volt regulator on it will send 5 volts to the Arduino. All other electronics and components attach onto this main body of the PDB, ESCs and Arduino. Our ESCs receive PWM signals which will control how much average voltage the motors connected to them will receive, which vary based on the orientation of the drone to keep it level. The Arduino is the brain of the drone and will control all the drones actions. Our LiPo battery is what powers the whole drone. The motors are A2212 1000KV motors which our propellors get attached to. The MPU-6050 IMU is a device with a gyroscope and accelerometer this is used to measure the orientation of the drone. The sonar sensor is used to measure distance and will be specifically mounted on the bottom of the drone to measure height.

**Programming:**
In our research we learned we needed to develop something called a Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) controller. Our initial design was a P controller and our newest design is a PD controller. The Proportional (P) part uses measurements from the MPU to generate a PWM signal to communicate to ESCs, which then give the motors a certain voltage generating just the right amount of thrust for a correction. For example if the MPU detects a 15 degrees tilt to the left the P part of the controller will set a correction PWM signal for the motor of 15, but this value is scaled using a constant called Kp, because the raw value of 15 could undershoot or overshoot the correction needed. This constant needs to be tuned through testing. The derivative portion helps measure how fast the drone is correcting itself and helps stop the drone from over shooting a correction. This is done by finding the rate of change of orientation error from level. For example the MPU measures a 15 degrees tilt to the left and half a second later it measures a 10 degree tilt. The rate of change is 10 degrees/second, and this can be used in the PWM signal calculation as well to stop the correction from over shooting and creating an oscillating drone, it can lower the corrections if the drone is already rotating towards level, as this values is subtracted or added from the PWM signal calculated by the P portion based on the direction of motion such as the angle increasing or decreasing. This value also needs to be tuned through testing, we call it kd. One thing we added to our drone is a state machine, which updates the drone’s mode and target automatically based on its measured height above the ground using the ultrasonic sensor. The states are Ground, Rise, Hover, Descend, and Land. Each state determines what the drone should be doing at a given point in the flight, allowing the drone to progress through takeoff, hovering, and landing autonomously.


**Hardware:**
The Hardware consists of a PDB with a 5 Volt regulator on it that allows us to solder wires to it that can later become a 5V input for the Arduino. The other connection points on the PDB are for the ESCs and they will receive the full 11.1V from the LiPo battery, and then the ESCs attach each to a brushless motor. The Arduino has the low voltage electronics attached to it, like the MPU, Sonar and IR receiver. Everything in this setup has a common ground including the ESCs as no only do they have wires attached to the PDB, but also a signal input wire and ground wire connected to the Arduino circuit. To make having a common ground we initially used a bread board that the Arduino ground and all other electronic grounds could plug into, but this took up too much space. So we began using another connector that does the same job, but is more flexible to use as it isn't just one breadboard but connecter pieces that can help put up to 3 wires together to have a common ground/voltage. These are called WAGO connectors, and they helped all our electronics stay as parallel branch connections maintaining that essential common ground.

**Frame design:**
We have had two prototypes for our drone. Our initial idea was a 3D printed PLA frame suggested and designed by friend as mentioned in the first summary, however we did not have access to a 3D printer so we had to improvise with common materials we had. The first frame we made was with extra Legos; we had made measurements about how large the center body had to be to fit all the components, this came out to 15X15cm. We also determined how long the arms would have to be to stop out 10(diameter inches)x4.5(pitch inches) propellors from hitting each other, our first design had the motors (also the center placement of the propellors) 19cm away from the body. Which was a generous amount of extra space. This frame was built and went through multiple tests unitl it broke. Our second frame was more compact and had the motors only 14cm away from the body which reduced the torque experienced at the connection joint, this frame was also made of plywood and used screws and bolts to put everything together. This frame is much stronger and has held up to all tests. 

**Mentor Advice:**
Once we were ready for running some tests to gather data about the drone we first went to a mentor I had met through the UBC mentorship program to ask for advice on our drone. What he had strongly recommended to us was to design a kill switch as well just incase anything ever went wrong in testing, and to make a physical switch that can turn the drone on and off rather then having to remove the battery connection every time as it can be quite a hassle, and even damage components. We were glad we talked to him before testing, with propellors on, and grateful for his advice. As it did end up saving us a couple times. The kill switch was installed right away. It was a software kill switch that involved an IR receiver and remote I had lying around in an electronics kit. The IR receiver would constantly check if the remote power button had been clicked every loop, and once clicked would send the motors to their minimum throttle(off) right away, and no other code would run again until the program was restarted. Allowing us to safely handle the drone and disconnect power with out worrying about the propellors spinning. The power switch we are still designing and finding a way to incorporate. 

**Prototyping and experimenting:**
For our tests we ran multiple smaller programs to verify all functions of the drone worked. This included an MPU test(1), sonar test(2), ESCs  + Motor test(3), a combined MPU and ESCs test(4), a hover test(5) and currently a PD controller tuning test(6). 

**(1)** The MPU test was done with the Arduino, its serial monitor and MPU. In this test I would rotate the MPU and look at the serial monitor to see what readings it was making, and more importantly checked that it was generating the correct correction PWM signals to send to the ESCs. 

**(2)** The sonar test involved the Arduino, ultrasonic sensor and state machine code. With this test I would adjust the sonar height and read the serial monitor which was programmed to display the current mode of the drone to make sure the drone was going into the correct mode at the correct position. 

**(3)** After both these test were a success the ESCs + Motor test was conducted where all we wanted to see was the motors spin, we sent one constant PWM signal to the ESCs and saw all motors spinning smoothly and consistently, we also used this test to find out what the minimum PWM signal was needed to spin the motors, it came out to 1075. This test like all the other was done with out the propellors, this was also the test we ensured the kill switch worked by checking that the motors would stop when we clicked the remotes power button. 

**(4)** Our final non-propellor test was a combined MPU,ESCs and motors test. Here we mounted everything on the drone and began tilting it with the motors constantly running. We ran the program on the minimum PWM signal needed for motor spin so when the drone was tiled and PWM signals were added and subtracted from both sides based on the orientation the motors that had the signal subtracted would turn off. This allowed us to make sure we placed our MPU correctly compared to how out motors were listed in our code, by seeing that the correct motors would turn off when the drone was tilted a certain way. 

**(5)** After this success we went back to the ESCs + Kill switch only code, but with propellors on to test multiple PWM signals to see which one started get our drone mostly off the ground to roughly estimate which PWM signal corresponded with hover throttle. This is where hit our first failed attempt, we made the PWM signal increments of increase too large and then on one test our Lego frame was torn apart, as the torque generated by the propellors was too strong for the body-arm connection to handle. This is when we made our plywood frame with shorter stronger arms, both adjustments to allow it to hold up against the torque. The motor placement was now 5cm closer reducing the moment at the arm-body connection, and the addition of the thicker plywood arm made the drone very sturdy. This drone has held up against all PWM signals we've tested quite easily and is still light enough to fly. 

**(6)** Now we are currently tuning our PD controller this is done by having only one axis of the drone actively adjusting itself on a PWM signal that is a little less than the one needed for hovering. We hang the drone in this configuration from strings and lean it back and forth with the axis that is actively adjusting itself to stay balanced to tune the Kp and Kd values mentioned earlier. So that drone gets back to level when we tilt it but doesn't overshoot and begin oscillating.
